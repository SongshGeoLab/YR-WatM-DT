# Yellow River Basin Water Resource Management Digital Twin System - Storyline

## I. Background: The Fifth-Longest River, Yet Scarce in Water and Rich in Sediment

In this digital twin case study, our research area is the Yellow River Basin in northern China. This is the world's fifth-longest river, yet it has a disproportionately small catchment area and river discharge relative to its length (see Page 1, left panel, tab 1). This mismatch makes the Yellow River a unique water resource management challenge: although water is scarce, the river is exceptionally rich in sediment, making it extremely difficult to manage (see Page 1, left panel, tab 2).

The root cause of the high sediment load lies in thousands of years of intensive human activities and development. Continuous agricultural expansion has reduced the basin's forest coverage, leading to increasingly severe soil erosion throughout history. Through our historical data analysis, you can see the dramatic changes in sediment load: at its peak, the Yellow River's sediment discharge was almost comparable to that of the Amazon River, despite having much lower water flow. This massive sediment accumulation reduces downstream channel stability, continuously raises riverbeds, and leads to more frequent flood disasters. For this reason, many historical dynasties were deeply troubled by Yellow River flood management, earning it the reputation as "China's Sorrow."

## II. Historical Achievement: Reforestation and Flood Control Have Solved the Sediment Problem

However, this millennium-old challenge has seen breakthrough progress in recent decades. In recent years, the central government has conducted large-scale "Grain for Green" programs in the middle reaches to reduce downstream flood risks, significantly decreasing soil erosion. From our monitoring data (Page 1, right panel), you can see that forest coverage has significantly recovered from its historical low point. Combined with soil conservation, terrace construction, and other management measures, sediment levels have gradually returned to relatively stable conditions.

Based on our multi-source analysis integrating observations, data, and models, these human activities, particularly land use changes, have dramatically altered the entire basin's water cycle processes, including precipitation, evapotranspiration, and runoff at every stage. It can be said that through decades of sustained efforts, the sediment problem has been effectively controlled—this is a major historical achievement in Yellow River governance.

But solving the "sediment" problem does not mean the Yellow River's challenges are over.

## III. The Water Challenge Remains Critical: Historical Water Stress Has Been Persistently High

In fact, water scarcity is a deeper and more persistent challenge for the Yellow River Basin. From our Water Stress Index (Page 7), we can see that the Yellow River Basin's water stress has historically remained at high levels, far exceeding the internationally recognized safety threshold of 0.4, and often approaching the critical warning line of 0.6. This means that even though the sediment problem has been solved, the Yellow River Basin's water security remains precarious.

What's more concerning is that even considering climate change impacts, the problem will not resolve itself. While under different climate scenarios, future water availability will increase due to enhanced glacier melt and precipitation changes (Page 2, right panel), if we take no proactive interventions and rely solely on natural changes—focusing only on economic development while neglecting water resource management—the Yellow River Basin's water stress will remain above the warning line, unable to meet the dual needs of ecological protection and social development.

This raises a critical question: What policy measures do we need to implement to achieve sustainable water resource utilization while ensuring economic development?

## IV. Three Current National Policies: South-North Water Transfer, Clean Energy, and Universal Water Conservation

To address these challenges, three core national policies vigorously promoted by the Chinese government in recent years provide important policy tools for Yellow River Basin water resource management:

### 1. South-North Water Transfer: Increasing Water Supply and Ensuring Ecological Baselines

The first policy is the South-North Water Transfer Project. The Chinese government is planning and implementing this grand inter-basin water diversion strategy to transport large quantities of water from the Yangtze River in the south to the Yellow River Basin, fundamentally enhancing future water resource availability.

The significance of the South-North Water Transfer lies not only in meeting human production and domestic water demands, but more importantly, in immediately addressing ecological problems and ensuring ecological flow requirements (Page 6). According to expert estimates, the Yellow River requires at least 1.74 billion cubic meters of ecological flow annually to ensure basic ecological functions such as sediment balance, wetland ecology, and fish spawning. Without the South-North Water Transfer supplementation, even under optimal water resource management scenarios, the Yellow River Basin can barely pass the test and cannot achieve an ideal ecological security state.

Of course, this inter-basin water diversion will also have certain impacts on southern basins, so relevant planning remains under cautious preparation. However, it's worth noting that the Eastern and Middle Route projects have already been launched and are operational, with water diversion volumes increasing progressively. So far, results have been positive, providing strong support for the Yellow River Basin's water security.

### 2. Clean Energy: Reducing Industrial Water Use and Promoting Green Transformation

The second policy is vigorously developing clean energy. This may seem unrelated to water resource management, but in fact, the connection is close.

As China's industrialization level continues to improve, industrial water demand continues to rise (Page 4). A significant portion comes from cooling water for traditional thermal power generation. Therefore, this water consumption will be directly influenced by the future proportion of thermal power in China's energy structure. As is well known, China is leading the global renewable energy revolution, vigorously promoting wind and solar energy to replace traditional coal power.

Currently, China's thermal power proportion is approximately 60%. According to national energy transition plans, reducing it to 40% is completely achievable. If it decreases further to 30% or even lower, industrial water consumption will significantly decrease. Our model analysis shows (Page 5) that increasing the proportion of clean energy has the most significant impact on when regional water use peaks.

Since water demand decreases in other sectors (agricultural water conservation, population peaking) exceed industrial sector water demand increases, total water consumption in our region will inevitably peak at some point. According to different policy parameter combinations, our model predicts peak timing generally between 2035 and 2050. Accelerating clean energy development can significantly advance this peak timing, gaining precious time for ecological recovery.

### 3. Universal Water Conservation: Comprehensive Improvement of Water Use Efficiency from Agriculture to Daily Life

The third policy is promoting universal water conservation, a key measure for demand-side water resource management.

Let's examine future overall water resource demand predictions (Page 5). Among four water use categories, only industrial water will grow, while others will gradually decrease. Agricultural irrigation has always been the most water-consuming sector, accounting for over 60% of total water use. The good news is that irrigation efficiency is continuously improving, and irrigation water demand is steadily decreasing.

Currently, the Yellow River Basin's water-saving irrigation ratio, including hardened channel irrigation, drip irrigation, and pipe irrigation technologies, has reached around 80%, an absolutely world-leading level. While future technical optimization space is limited, combined with gradual farmland area reduction and crop structure adjustments, future irrigation water consumption still has potential to decrease by approximately 1.16 billion cubic meters.

Regarding domestic water use (Page 3), although population will peak around 2030 due to declining fertility rates, China's current dietary structure consumes large amounts of meat and protein, with livestock and aquaculture water demand not peaking until around 2050. Here lies an important policy space: if we simultaneously encourage people to adopt more balanced, plant-based dietary structures, water consumption increases from population growth can be greatly reduced.

This analysis also demonstrates that population policies being promoted by the government will not be significantly constrained by water resources—the key lies in guiding reasonable consumption patterns.

## V. Future Outlook: Promising to Balance Development and Ecology

So what kind of future can these three policies bring?

Our digital twin system has conducted systematic analysis of different policy combinations for future scenarios (Page 7). Results show that different policy intensities will directly determine when the Yellow River Basin achieves water security:

**Conservative Scenario**: If we implement no aggressive policy measures and merely maintain the status quo, the Water Stress Index won't fall below the 0.4 warning line until late this century—clearly unable to meet ecological protection and high-quality development needs.

**Aggressive Scenario**: If we simultaneously adopt the most aggressive water resource management policies—large-scale South-North Water Transfer, rapid energy transition, and strict water conservation measures—the timeline for the Water Stress Index to drop to the safety line could advance to the 2030s, though this might create considerable pressure on economic development and southern basins.

**Balanced Scenario**: A more realistic middle path that balances economic development with sustainability suggests comprehensive achievement of water and ecological security in the Yellow River Basin by the 2040s. Under this scenario, the three policies work progressively and synergistically, ensuring both stable economic development and gradual ecological recovery.

This is precisely why we developed this Decision Theater system. It provides policymakers with a powerful tool to intuitively see how different policy combinations impact the future, enabling them to find the most suitable policy pathway. With the synergistic effects of the South-North Water Transfer, clean energy, and universal water conservation policies, we have every reason to believe that the Yellow River Basin can balance development and ecology in the future, achieving truly sustainable development and transforming this once "River of Sorrow" into a "River of Happiness."
